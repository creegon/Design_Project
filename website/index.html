Source Attribution of
Watermarked Text

When multiple LLMs independently deploy watermarks, who produced what? We compare cryptographic watermark-based attribution with statistical fingerprint-based attribution across four LLMs and uncover a robustness crossover — watermarks dominate at low attack intensity, fingerprints win once token perturbation becomes severe.

Report & Poster Results Summary

Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed with cryptographic watermarks that let the model provider prove a piece of text was generated by its system. When multiple providers each ship their own watermark, a natural question arises: given an arbitrary piece of text, which model produced it? This is the source-attribution problem.

Two attribution paradigms exist in isolation. Watermark attribution (active, cryptographic) assigns each model a unique KGW key and runs a z-score hypothesis test against every candidate key. Fingerprint attribution (passive, statistical) trains a TF-IDF classifier on each model's natural distributional signature. They have never been compared head-to-head, and the interaction between the two paradigms under adversarial attack is open.

We conduct a unified study with four open-weight LLMs (LLaMA-3.2-1B, LLaMA-3.2-3B, Gemma-2B, Phi-2) under KGW watermarking (γ = 0.25, δ = 2.0, z-threshold = 3.0), 30 samples per model, and a five-axis attack suite (synonym substitution 10–70%, random insert/delete 10–30%, 100% sentence reorder). We report three findings: (i) watermarking compresses inter-model distributional differences, (ii) watermark attribution is cliff-like — strong until it collapses, and (iii) a TF-IDF fingerprint baseline degrades gracefully, crossing over the watermark curve in the heavy-attack regime. A secondary study (Task 2) quantifies watermark decay across real generator → paraphraser chains.

Executive Summary

This design project addresses the source-attribution problem for watermarked large-language-model output: when several LLMs each deploy their own cryptographic watermark, can an observer correctly identify which model produced a given text — and does that answer survive adversarial paraphrasing?

  • Distributional compression. A controlled comparison of Gemma-2B vs. Phi-2 shows that independent KGW watermarks shrink the inter-model distributional gap (weighted diff 0.24) relative to clean output (0.76) — a previously undocumented side-effect with direct implications for attribution.
  • Watermark attribution is strong but brittle. Per-model KGW keys achieve 96.7% single-model attribution and 88.5% mixed-detection accuracy on clean text, but collapse to 13.3% under 70% synonym substitution.
  • Robustness crossover. A TF-IDF n-gram fingerprint baseline degrades gracefully from 76.7% (clean) to 63.3% (70% synonym). The two curves cross between 50–70% attack intensity — watermarks dominate below the crossover, fingerprints dominate above it.
  • Multi-LLM chain study (Task 2). 17 generator → paraphraser experiments (Llama-3.2-3B → Qwen-3-4B / 3-8B) confirm that KGW does not survive a real neural paraphraser: only 1 / 17 (6%) of texts retained detectable watermark signal, with an average z-score decay of 5.07.
  • Takeaway. Watermark and fingerprint attribution have non-overlapping failure modes; any production attribution pipeline should combine the two.

Problem & Research Gaps

The source-attribution problem

Multiple LLMs now embed watermarks in their generated text for provenance, copyright, and disinformation control. When an observer receives a piece of text, they need to answer: which model produced it? Doing so reliably — especially when the text has been rewritten, shortened, or otherwise adversarially perturbed — is the source-attribution problem.

Gaps in the literature

1
Distributional side-effects of watermarking

Does embedding a watermark make different models more similar to one another (by suppressing each model's natural distribution in favor of the watermark's green-list bias)? No prior work measures this.

2
Watermark vs. fingerprint, head-to-head

Watermark-based attribution (active, cryptographic) and fingerprint-based attribution (passive, statistical) have never been evaluated on a shared testbed with identical samples, models, and attacks.

3
Robustness under adversarial attack

Is a watermark signal more or less robust than a distributional fingerprint when an attacker rewrites the text? The answer determines which paradigm a production pipeline should trust.

Our contributions

  1. A quantitative measurement of how KGW watermarking compresses inter-model distributional differences.
  2. The first head-to-head comparison of KGW watermark attribution vs. TF-IDF fingerprint attribution under a unified 5-axis attack suite.
  3. Discovery of a robustness crossover: the two paradigms fail in non-overlapping regimes, motivating hybrid attribution.
  4. A 17-sample multi-LLM chain-paraphrase benchmark (Task 2) that quantifies watermark decay across real generator → paraphraser pipelines.

Method — Two Attribution Paradigms

Background — KGW Watermarking

Both attribution paradigms in this study assume the underlying watermark is KGW (Kirchenbauer et al., ICML 2023). KGW embeds a signal by biasing token-level logits according to a pseudo-random green/red partition of the vocabulary. The generation & detection pipeline has five steps:

  1. 1
    Seed from preceding token. Hash the previous token with a secret key to obtain a per-step seed.
  2. 2
    Partition the vocabulary. The seed deterministically splits the vocabulary into a Green list (γ = 0.25, ≈ 25% of tokens) and a Red list (≈ 75%).
  3. 3
    Bias the logits. Add δ = 2.0 to every green-list token's logit before softmax.
  4. 4
    Sample the next token. Draw from the biased distribution, pushing generation toward green tokens without hard-blocking red ones.
  5. 5
    Detect via z-test. Count green tokens in the received text, compute a z-score against the null hypothesis (natural green rate = γ). If z > 3.0 → the text carries the watermark.

Reference: Kirchenbauer, J., Geiping, J., Wen, Y., Katz, J., Miers, I., & Goldstein, T. A Watermark for Large Language Models. ICML 2023 · arXiv:2301.10226

Results

Finding 1 — Watermarking Compresses Distributional Differences

A controlled three-setting comparison of Gemma-2B vs. Phi-2 isolates the effect of watermarking on inter-model distributional gap. Larger values = more distinguishable models.

# Setting Weighted diff Entropy diff Top-10 conc. Green-rate diff Distinguishability
1 Independent WM, different green lists 0.2386 0.7573 0.0499 0.0050 Weak–Med
2 No WM, independent green lists (labels only) 0.7611 2.2990 0.2321 0.0118 Strong
3 No WM, shared green list 1.5594 4.4836 0.2712 0.7220 Very Strong

Interpretation: Watermarking (row 1) collapses every distributional metric to roughly 1/3 of the clean baseline (row 2). Green-list bias dominates the model's natural signature, making watermarked Gemma and watermarked Phi look more alike — a direct headwind for passive fingerprint attribution.

⭐ Finding 3 — Robustness Crossover: Watermark vs. Fingerprint

Running the TF-IDF fingerprint baseline on the same attack suite reveals that the two paradigms fail in non-overlapping regimes. Watermark accuracy collapses cliff-like once token-level perturbation becomes heavy; fingerprint accuracy degrades gracefully because lexical statistics survive synonym substitution better than individual green-token membership.

Attribution accuracy vs. synonym-substitution intensity — lines cross between 50%–70%

Sentence reorder 100%76.7%96.7%Watermark

Why do they cross?

Watermark = token-level signal

Every token carries green/red membership. Synonym substitution directly destroys the signal because each replacement token is drawn from the full vocabulary, ignoring the green list. Result: cliff-like collapse at high perturbation.

Fingerprint = distributional signal

TF-IDF captures aggregate n-gram frequency patterns. Synonyms tend to be drawn from semantically-neighboring parts of the vocabulary, so the distribution is partially preserved. Result: graceful degradation.

Takeaway. A real neural paraphraser is dramatically more destructive to KGW than symbolic synonym substitution — even with an initial z-score of 5.7+, the signal decays by an average of 5.07 and inverts sign in 10 / 17 cases. Spring 2026 — SimMark & Hybrid Watermarking

Task 2 revealed that KGW alone cannot survive neural paraphrasing. In the second semester, we integrated SimMark (sentence-level semantic watermarking) and built a Hybrid KGW+SimMark system that embeds both signals simultaneously.

SimMark — Sentence-Level Semantic Watermarking

Unlike KGW (token-level), SimMark embeds watermark signals in the statistical patterns of inter-sentence similarity. During generation, rejection sampling ensures consecutive sentence pairs have cosine similarity within a target interval. Because paraphrasing preserves semantic content, the similarity patterns survive moderate rewriting.

Token-level

KGW

  • Green/red list partition per token
  • Logit bias δ = 2.0 on green tokens
  • Detection: count green tokens → z-test
  • Strength: works on short text (≤3 sentences)
  • Weakness: synonym substitution destroys signal
Sentence-level

SimMark

Aghdam et al., EMNLP 2025 · arXiv:2502.02787

  • Rejection sampling on sentence-pair cosine similarity
  • Target interval: (0.75, 0.83) for Llama
  • Detection: soft count of in-interval pairs → z-test
  • Strength: survives moderate paraphrasing (semantic preserved)
  • Weakness: needs ≥4 sentences; fails on aggressive compression

However, under aggressive paraphrase (sentence compression 12→6 sentences), KGW survived (z = 4.18) while SimMark failed (z = 0.78) — sentence merging destroys inter-sentence similarity patterns. The two methods are complementary.

Hybrid KGW+SimMark — Architecture

We combine KGW and SimMark into a single generation pipeline. Each token is generated with KGW's green-list bias; after completing each sentence, the SimMark similarity constraint is checked. If unsatisfied, the sentence is rejected and regenerated — ensuring both watermark signals are embedded simultaneously.

Generation
KGW LogitsProcessor + per-sentence SimMark rejection sampling (max 50 retries)
Detection
OR logic (recommended): KGW z > 3.0 or SimMark z > 2.0 → watermarked

Discussion

Watermark: token-level signal

  • Each token carries green / red membership assigned by a PRF keyed on the previous token.
  • Synonym substitution directly destroys this membership, since replacements are drawn from the full vocabulary.
  • Failure is cliff-like: accuracy holds until a threshold, then collapses.
  • Good for: clean traffic, compliance scenarios, provenance stamping.

Fingerprint: distributional signal

  • TF-IDF aggregates many n-gram frequencies; no single token is load-bearing.
  • Synonyms typically live in semantically-adjacent parts of the vocabulary, so global statistics are partially preserved.
  • Failure is gradual: each attack shaves a few percentage points.
  • Good for: adversarial regimes, robustness under paraphrase.

Complementarity

  • Watermark: high accuracy, brittle failure.
  • Fingerprint: moderate accuracy, graceful degradation.
  • Their failure modes are non-overlapping — they fail on different inputs.
  • A hybrid classifier combining both signals should outperform either alone across the full attack spectrum.

Limitations & Future Work

Limitations

  • Model coverage. Tested on two model families (LLaMA, Gemma) plus Phi-2; broader validation on Mistral, Qwen, Claude-family open weights is needed.
  • Attack coverage. Neural paraphrasing attacks (GPT rewrite, Claude rewrite) are not yet included in the attribution benchmark — Task 2 hints at their severity.
  • Key secrecy assumption. The watermark attribution paradigm assumes the per-model keys remain private. Fingerprint attribution is unaffected by key leaks.
  • Sample size. 30 samples per model is sufficient for relative comparison but statistical power on narrow-margin cases (e.g. 50% synonym) is limited.

Future Directions

  1. Hybrid attribution classifier. Feed both KGW z-scores and TF-IDF features into a single classifier, exploiting the non-overlapping failure modes.
  2. Neural paraphrasing attacks. Extend the attack suite with GPT-4 / Claude rewrite, back-translation, and topic-preserving summarization.
  3. More model families. Add Mistral-7B, Qwen-2.5, and a closed-source proxy (Claude-Haiku / GPT-4o-mini distilled samples).
  4. Watermark-aware fingerprints. Train the fingerprint classifier on watermarked data so it learns to distinguish models despite the distributional compression found in Finding 1.
